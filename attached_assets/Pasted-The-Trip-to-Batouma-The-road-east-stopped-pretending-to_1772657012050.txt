The Trip to Batouma
The road east stopped pretending to be a road an hour outside the city.
It didn’t end so much as degrade—pavement dissolving into patched asphalt, asphalt giving way to hard-packed dirt where the tires found their own lanes, carving grooves through red earth. The Land Cruiser rocked and groaned like it resented being asked to carry anything heavier than dust.
Miranda sat in the back seat because there were only so many places you could put tension and still call it seating. Ben drove with both hands welded to the wheel, shoulders locked, eyes scanning not just the road but the edges of it, as if the scrub could suddenly sprout rifles. Martin rode shotgun, leaning forward when Ben swerved around craters big enough to swallow a tire, then settling back as if he’d been born on roads like this.
Noah sat beside Miranda, too still.
He’d been quiet since they left the hotel. Quiet the way a fuse got quiet right before it went.
The air in the vehicle smelled like stale sweat and sun-baked plastic. The AC worked in brief, sputtering gulps, then fell behind again. Miranda’s shirt clung to her spine. Her thighs stuck to the seat. Every jolt made her teeth click together.
Thirteen hours, Martin had said. Thirteen hours one way.
Miranda had nodded like it was nothing. Like she hadn’t spent most of her adult life measuring distance in minutes to backup, minutes to a perimeter, minutes until something went wrong.
Thirteen hours was not a distance. It was an environment.
She checked her phone out of habit—her own phone, the one that still had her mother’s last text sitting unread at the top because she couldn’t handle it right now. No signal.
Ben hit another rut and the vehicle lurched. Miranda’s shoulder bumped Noah’s. He didn’t react. He didn’t even blink.
“You eat?” she asked, because silence was becoming its own weapon.
Noah’s eyes stayed forward. “Not hungry.”
“You should anyway.”
A faint, humorless exhale. “Is this you taking care of me, Agent Cruz?”
“DELTA,” she corrected before she could stop herself, and immediately hated how it sounded. Like a badge. Like a collar. Like he’d put a word around her throat and tugged.
Noah’s mouth twitched—not quite a smile. “Right.”
She swallowed her irritation. The job didn’t care what she hated. The job didn’t care what she wanted.
The sat phone Langford had issued sat in her bag, heavy as a verdict. She could feel it even when she wasn’t touching it.
Daily check-ins. Factual. No advocacy. No attachment.
She looked at Noah’s profile—sharp jaw, faint shadow of stubble, eyes that never looked anywhere accidentally. He didn’t seem like a man who needed anyone to be attached to him.
And still—
Miranda forced her gaze away. Outside, the landscape stretched flat and endless, green-brown scrub punctured by acacia and occasional clusters of mud-brick homes that looked like they’d been placed by a hand that didn’t care about roads.
A motorcycle passed them in the opposite direction with three people on it and a chicken wedged between two knees, its feet tied together, wings pressed against its body. The driver didn’t glance at them. The passenger did, his eyes flicking over the vehicle, over the faces, quick and flat. Information. Then gone.
Miranda’s stomach tightened.
“Stop staring at everyone like that,” Ben muttered without turning his head.
“I’m not staring.”
“You’re doing that thing,” Ben said. “Like you’re trying to memorize their teeth.”
Martin looked back over his shoulder. “She’s making sure we don’t get followed.”
Ben snorted. “We’re in the only Land Cruiser with tinted windows and American posture. If someone wants to follow us, they don’t need her permission.”
Miranda bristled. “American posture.”
“You sit like you’re waiting for HR to call you in,” Ben said. “Relax before you dislocate something.”
Noah’s voice cut in, quiet. “Ben.”
Ben shut up. Not sulky. Just immediate compliance, as if the word itself had weight.
Miranda filed it away. One more invisible thread she could feel but couldn’t see.
They drove.
The day burned upward, became afternoon, bled into late afternoon. The road narrowed in places where erosion had eaten its shoulders away. Ben slowed at each blind rise. Martin called out hazards in a calm voice, like a spotter in a rally race.
Miranda tried to track time by sun angle, by the tightening in her lower back, by the way the inside of her mouth tasted like stale coffee and nerves. She tried to ignore the itch between her shoulder blades that felt like eyes.
Paranoia was contagious in confined spaces. It jumped from breath to breath, from silence to silence, until even a bird lifting from a tree felt like a signal.
At a checkpoint—nothing more than a rope and two men with rifles and the authority that came from not being questioned—Ben slowed. Martin leaned out his window, spoke in French, and handed over something Miranda didn’t see.
The man with the rifle stared into the vehicle. His eyes hit Noah. Paused. Skated to Miranda. Paused again.
Miranda kept her face neutral, her hands visible. She did what she’d done a thousand times in rooms full of men who wanted something from her: she made herself boring.
The man’s gaze lingered on her throat, where sweat darkened the collar of her shirt. Then he looked away, signaled with his chin, and the rope dropped.
They rolled forward.
Miranda didn’t breathe until the checkpoint was behind them and the road swallowed them again.
“Why’d he look at me like that?” she asked, too sharply.
Martin’s expression didn’t change. “Because you’re not supposed to be here.”
“That’s not an answer.”
“It is,” Martin said. “It’s just not comforting.”
Noah shifted slightly beside her. “They’re watching for patterns. This isn’t a tourist route.”
“And we look like—what?” Miranda said.
Ben’s laugh was short. “We look like a problem.”
Miranda stared out the window. The light was turning honeyed now, throwing long shadows from the trees like bars across the earth.
Thirteen hours. An environment.
Her phone buzzed faintly—one bar of signal flickering, then dying. Nothing came through. Just a reminder that she had obligations waiting for her in a world that wasn’t here.
She thought of Langford’s voice. Tight. Controlled. Angry in the way men got when control slipped.
She thought of Bill’s blood. The sound it made. The way the world had narrowed down to the shock of it, the impossibility.
She pressed her forehead against the glass for a second. The heat seared her skin through the window. She pulled back.
Noah noticed. “You okay?”
The question landed wrong. Not because it wasn’t polite. Because it sounded like he was checking equipment.
Miranda’s jaw tightened. “Fine.”
He didn’t argue. He didn’t press. He just nodded, as if “fine” was a status indicator that let him proceed.
She hated that she wanted him to argue.
The sun fell. The world cooled. The sky deepened into a bruised purple that made the horizon look unreal. Ben clicked on the headlights, which immediately illuminated a hundred tiny insects that threw themselves into the beams like they were desperate to become part of the vehicle.
Miranda’s eyes burned. She realized she hadn’t blinked in too long.
Martin checked his own phone. Still dead. He tossed it onto the dash with a soft curse.
“No comms,” he said.
Noah’s gaze stayed forward. “We knew that.”
“We knew it in theory,” Martin replied. “In practice it means if we get hit out here, it’s just us.”
Ben’s knuckles went whiter on the wheel. “Love that.”
Miranda reached into her bag and touched the sat phone. Cold plastic. A little embossed seal. The feeling of Langford’s hand on the back of her neck even across oceans.
She hadn’t checked in today.
She’d told herself she’d do it once they stopped. Once there was signal. Once the world made sense again.
But the world didn’t offer “once.” The world offered now.
She pulled the sat phone out, thumb hovering over the keypad. Noah’s eyes flicked to it.
“Don’t,” he said softly.
Miranda froze. “Don’t what?”
“Don’t check in from the road,” Noah said. “Not here.”
“You don’t get to tell me how to do my job.”
Noah’s gaze slid to hers, calm and flat. “I’m not telling you how to do your job. I’m telling you this route is dirty. If someone’s watching, that phone is a flare.”
Martin didn’t look back, but his voice softened. “He’s right, Miranda.”
Miranda stared at them both. “My obligation is daily.”
Noah’s voice didn’t rise. “Then do it when we stop. In a place we control.”
“You keep saying ‘we,’” she said. “Like I’m—”
Like I belong to you, she didn’t finish. Like I’ve been absorbed into your orbit.
Noah’s expression didn’t change, but something in his eyes tightened. “You’re with us.”
That was the problem.
Miranda slid the sat phone back into her bag like she was holstering a weapon she couldn’t afford to use yet.
They drove on.
At some point, the road became so broken Ben had to slow to a crawl, steering between stones and potholes like he was navigating a minefield. The vehicle bounced. Miranda’s head throbbed with each impact.
She tried to sleep. Her body refused.
Every time she closed her eyes, she saw the cell tower, the yellow mist, the bodies—images she hadn’t actually witnessed, but had absorbed from reports and photos and Sabrina’s voice describing things too clinically to be human.
She saw Bill’s face. The exact moment his eyes went wide, recognition and shock and something like apology in them, as if he was sorry for being in her way.
She opened her eyes again, breathing hard, and Noah was watching her.
Not staring. Just… present.
Miranda’s chest tightened. She hated being seen. She hated that part of her liked it.
“Why Batouma?” she asked, because if she didn’t talk she’d start screaming.
Martin answered without turning. “A set of coordinates keeps surfacing in the patterns. Batouma sits near a corridor we can’t ignore.”
“That’s still vague.”
“It’s meant to be,” Ben said.
Miranda’s temper snapped. “I’m not a child. I’m DELTA. I approve or deny what you do.”
Silence filled the vehicle like smoke.
Then Noah spoke, quietly. “You’re tired.”
“That’s your response?”
“You’re tired,” Noah repeated, voice even, and somehow it was worse because it was true. “And you’re angry because you want control. I get it.”
Miranda laughed once, sharp and humorless. “You get it.”
Noah didn’t rise to it. “I do.”
She wanted to ask him what it was like to live with certainty and dread braided together. She wanted to ask him how it felt to see disaster coming and still be helpless.
But she couldn’t. Because she couldn’t afford to want anything from him.
They stopped a little after midnight at a roadside compound that was less a place and more an arrangement: a tin-roofed structure, a yard enclosed by cinderblock, a generator coughing somewhere behind it. Two men came out to meet them, wary but not surprised, as if Martin’s calls had traveled ahead of them through channels Miranda didn’t have names for.
Ben parked inside the yard. The gate closed behind them.
Miranda stepped out and the night air hit her like water—cooler, heavy with the smell of dust and woodsmoke. Her legs shook when she put weight on them. She hadn’t realized how long she’d been bracing.
Martin spoke with the men in French. One of them pointed toward a back room.
Noah touched Miranda’s elbow lightly, guiding her toward the building. The gesture was careful. Not ownership. Not quite.
Still, her skin prickled where his fingers had been.
Inside, the room was bare except for a cot, a table, and a single bulb that buzzed with insects. A fan turned slowly, pushing warm air in circles. The walls were cracked plaster.
Noah set down his bag. “Five minutes,” he said to the others, and they left without comment.
Miranda didn’t like being alone with him. She liked it too much.
She pulled the sat phone out and stepped into the corner, angling her body away as if that could create privacy. She dialed Langford’s number from memory.
It rang once.
Then twice.
Then connected.
“Agent Cruz,” Langford said immediately, as if he’d been holding the line open. “Report.”
Miranda kept her voice flat. “Traveling. Limited comms. Principal secure. Team intact.”
“Location?”
Miranda glanced at Noah. He shook his head once, minimal.
“Region east of Bamako,” Miranda said. “En route to investigate possible follow-on.”
Langford exhaled through his nose. “You’re being deliberately imprecise.”
“Yes, sir.”
A beat. Then, softer, more dangerous: “I’ve received no confirmation that you are where you claim to be. Do you understand the problem that creates for me?”
Miranda felt her shoulders tense. “I understand.”
“Good. Keep it that way,” Langford said. “And Cruz—don’t forget what you are. You’re the record. You don’t get to choose the story.”
The line clicked dead.
Miranda stared at the sat phone for a second too long, then shoved it back into her bag like it burned.
Noah was watching her again, face unreadable.
“What?” she snapped, immediately regretting it.
He didn’t flinch. “He’s tightening the leash.”
Miranda laughed again, smaller this time. “You don’t know him.”
Noah’s expression stayed calm. “I know leashes.”
The words shouldn’t have landed. They did.
Miranda looked away, throat tight. She busied herself with her bag, rearranging items that didn’t need rearranging. She found a folded set of clothes she didn’t remember packing. A sealed protein bar. A small med kit.
Then her fingers brushed paper.
A manila envelope, heavy stock, the kind used for legal filings. Her name was written across it in block letters.
MIRANDA CRUZ.
Her pulse spiked.
She stared at it, then looked at Noah. “What is this?”
Noah didn’t move. “Open it.”
Miranda’s hands were suddenly clumsy. She tore the seal.
Inside was a single typed page.
Not official letterhead. No agency seal. Just plain paper, crisp, as if it had been printed yesterday—or ten years ago.
At the top: IF MIRANDA CRUZ IS KILLED IN ACTION
Her breath caught.
She read the first line and her vision tunneled.
It was an exit narrative. A story. A clean sequence of events written in a voice that knew how to make people stop asking questions. It was careful, detailed, down to the names of witnesses and the phrasing of statements. It framed her death as necessary, heroic, inevitable. It included instructions for who to call, what to say, what to send, where her body should be routed, how her mother should be notified, how to avoid a media storm.
There was a paragraph about Bill.
Miranda’s mouth went dry.
It didn’t absolve her. It didn’t accuse her. It placed his death in the past like a stone in a river, something the current had moved around.
At the bottom was a line: DELIVER TO LANGFORD IF RECOVERY IS POSSIBLE. IF NOT, BURN.
Miranda’s hands shook hard enough the paper fluttered like a trapped bird.
She looked up slowly.
Noah’s face was still. His eyes were tired.
“You wrote this,” she said, voice thin.
Noah didn’t deny it. “Yes.”
“When?”
Noah’s gaze held hers. “Before you got on the plane.”
Miranda felt something hot and ugly rise in her chest. “Before you even met me.”
“I’d met your file,” Noah said.
“That’s not meeting.”
“I don’t have the luxury of waiting,” Noah replied, and there was a quiet anger in it that made her step back. “People die around me. If I don’t prepare, more people die.”
Miranda’s voice cracked. “So I’m disposable. That’s what this is.”
Noah’s eyes flicked to the paper in her hands. “It’s not about disposal.”
“It sure as hell reads like it.”
Noah’s jaw tightened. “It’s about minimizing damage.”
“Damage,” she repeated, bitter. “Like I’m an asset.”
Noah didn’t correct her. And that—more than anything—hurt.
Miranda stared at the page again. At the neat, clean story of her death. At the way it made her feel both seen and erased.
A part of her wanted to tear it in half. A part of her wanted to keep it, because it meant he’d considered her real enough to plan for.
She hated that part of herself.
She folded the page with shaking hands and shoved it back into the envelope.
Noah watched her, expression unreadable. “You don’t have to stay.”
Miranda’s laugh was sharp and brittle. “You think I have a life to go back to?”
Silence.
Outside, the generator coughed. Somewhere, a dog barked once and went quiet.
Miranda forced her voice steady. “I stay because civilians die if I walk away.”
Noah nodded once, as if that was the only reason that mattered. “Then sleep.”
Miranda stared at him, anger and exhaustion swirling together. “You’re not even going to apologize.”
Noah’s eyes held hers. “For preparing?”
“For treating me like a—”
She stopped. Because she didn’t know what word hurt most: asset, tool, disposable, collateral.
Noah’s voice softened by a fraction. “I’m sorry it hurts.”
It wasn’t an apology. It was an acknowledgment. It was him telling her pain was part of the contract.
Miranda turned away before he could see her eyes burn.
She lay on the cot fully clothed. The thin mattress pressed springs into her spine. The fan did nothing. Her body vibrated with fatigue, but her mind stayed sharp, refusing to unclench.
Through the open doorway she saw Ben and Martin on the other side of the room, speaking low. Sabrina wasn’t here; she was a voice in their ears and a ghost behind their decisions.
Noah stood alone near the door, looking out into the yard as if listening to something in the dark.
Miranda closed her eyes.
For a moment, she almost drifted.
Then a vibration—soft, brief—cut through the room.
Ben’s burner phone, tossed on the table, lit up.
One line of text.
Miranda watched Ben pick it up, his face tightening as he read.
Noah turned instantly, as if he’d felt the change in air.
Ben looked at him. Swallowed.
Then held the phone out.
Noah read the message, and for the first time since she’d met him, his composure slipped—a flicker of something sharp and animal behind the eyes.
Miranda sat up, heart hammering. “What is it?”
Noah didn’t look away from the screen when he answered.
“Turn around.”
